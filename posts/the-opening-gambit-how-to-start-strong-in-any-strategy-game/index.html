The Opening Gambit: How to Start Strong in Any Strategy Game

2026-05-29 · Strategy Games · Tags: strategy games, boardgamereviews

Every strategy game tells a story, and the opening chapter sets the tone for everything that follows. Whether you're laying down your first settlement in Catan, moving your pawn to e4 in chess, or placing your initial workers in Terraforming Mars, the decisions you make in the first few turns can echo across the entire match. Many players treat the opening as a mere formality—a scripted sequence of moves they've memorized or a casual warm-up before the "real" game begins. But the truth is that the opening is where victories are forged and defeats are sealed. In this article, we'll explore why the opening matters so much, the principles that underpin a strong start, the common pitfalls that trip up even experienced players, and how to adapt your opening to the unique dynamics of your table. By the end, you'll see the first moves of any game not as a prelude, but as the foundation of your entire strategy.

The Opening: More Than Just the First Moves

In many strategy games, the opening is the phase where you have the most freedom and the least information. You know the board, the rules, and your own objectives, but you don't yet know what your opponents will do. This asymmetry creates a delicate balance between committing to a plan and staying flexible. A strong opening does three things: it establishes a position that gives you options, it creates pressure or opportunities that your opponents must respond to, and it sets the tempo of the game. Think of chess: the Italian Game and the Sicilian Defense are not just sequences of moves—they are philosophical statements about how you intend to fight. The same applies to modern eurogames. In Catan, your initial settlement placement determines your resource economy for the entire game. In Terraforming Mars, your starting corporation and cards shape your engine. The opening is not just about the first few turns; it's about the strategic direction you commit to.

Consider the concept of "tempo" in games like chess or Go. Tempo is the advantage of moving first or forcing your opponent to react. A well-played opening grabs tempo and never lets go. In a game like Twilight Struggle, the opening card plays can swing the balance of influence on the board, and a single misstep can give your opponent a foothold in a critical region. The opening is where you lay the tracks for the rest of the journey—if you lay them poorly, you'll spend the midgame trying to correct course, often at a steep cost.

The Core Principles of a Strong Opening

While every game has its own rules and mechanics, there are universal principles that apply to almost any strategy game's opening. These are not rigid rules but guiding lights that help you make sound decisions when the path isn't clear.

  • Control the center (or the key area): In many games, the center of the board offers the most mobility and influence. In chess, controlling the center allows your pieces to move freely. In Catan, the center of the board often has the best numbers and resources. In area-control games like El Grande, the center is where the scoring happens. Identify the "center" of your game—the region that gives you the most leverage—and fight for it early.
  • Develop efficiently: In games with multiple pieces or actions, you want to get your pieces into play as quickly as possible. In chess, that means developing knights and bishops before moving the queen. In worker-placement games, it means placing your workers on the most valuable spots before they're taken. Efficiency is about maximizing the value of each action, and the opening is where you have the most actions to spend.
  • Maintain flexibility: A common mistake is to over-commit to a single strategy too early. The best openings keep multiple options open. In a game like Scythe, you might start by building a structure that could support either an economic or military approach. In a card game like Magic: The Gathering, you might play a land and a creature that could go either aggro or control. Flexibility allows you to adapt to your opponents' moves and to the random elements of the game.
  • Create threats: A strong opening doesn't just develop your own position—it forces your opponents to respond. In chess, a threat like a fork or a discovered attack can give you tempo. In a game like Risk, placing armies on a border province threatens an invasion. By creating threats, you dictate the pace and make your opponents react to you, rather than the other way around.

These principles are not exhaustive, but they form the backbone of a solid opening. When you're unsure what to do, ask yourself: Am I controlling the key area? Am I developing efficiently? Am I staying flexible? Am I creating threats? If you can answer yes to at least two of these, you're on the right track.

Common Opening Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Even experienced players fall into traps during the opening. Recognizing these mistakes is the first step to avoiding them.

Mistake #1: Overextending. It's tempting to grab as much territory or as many resources as possible in the early game. But overextending leaves you vulnerable. In a game like Catan, placing settlements on scattered resources might give you variety, but it also spreads your presence thin, making it hard to build roads and cities. In a war game, pushing too far forward can stretch your supply lines. The fix: focus on a compact, defensible position that you can build upon. Quality over quantity.

Mistake #2: Ignoring your opponents. The opening is not a solitaire exercise. If you focus only on your own plan, you'll miss what your opponents are doing. In a game like Agricola, if you don't watch which resources your opponents are taking, you might find yourself blocked out of a critical action. In chess, if you ignore your opponent's threats, you'll lose material. The fix: after each of your moves, take a moment to assess the board from your opponents' perspective. What are they threatening? What are they building toward? Adjust your plan accordingly.

Mistake #3: Memorizing without understanding. Many players learn opening sequences by rote, but they don't understand the underlying reasons. When the game deviates from the script—and it always does—they're lost. The fix: learn the principles behind the moves. Why is that pawn move good? What does it accomplish? When you understand the "why," you can improvise when the situation changes.

Mistake #4: Being too passive. Some players play defensively in the opening, waiting to see what others do. But in most games, the player who seizes the initiative has a significant advantage. Passivity allows your opponents to dictate the game. The fix: look for opportunities to make aggressive but sound moves. Even in a peaceful eurogame, you can be aggressive by grabbing a key resource or blocking a valuable spot.

Mistake #5: Neglecting the endgame. The opening should be played with the endgame in mind. Every move you make should contribute to your long-term goals. If you're playing a game with scoring conditions, think about how your opening sets up those conditions. In a game like Dominion, your first few buys determine your deck's engine. In a game like 7 Wonders, your starting wonder and cards shape your strategy for all three ages. The fix: before you make your first move, have a rough idea of how you intend to win. The opening is the first step on that path.

Reading the Table: Adapting Your Opening to Opponents

No two games are alike because no two groups of players are alike. The opening that works against a cautious player might fail against an aggressive one. Adapting your opening to your opponents is a skill that separates good players from great ones.

First, consider the player count. In a two-player game, the opening is a direct duel—every move you make is aimed at your opponent. In a four-player game, the dynamics are more complex; you have to consider alliances, kingmaking, and the fact that your opponents are also competing with each other. In a game like Catan, the opening placement is even more critical with more players because the board fills up quickly. In a game like Diplomacy, the opening is all about signaling and negotiation.

Second, consider your opponents' play styles. Are they aggressive or passive? Do they prefer to build engines or to attack? If you know that a player tends to hoard resources, you might want to block their access to a key resource early. If a player is known for rushing a particular strategy, you can prepare a counter. In a game like Terraforming Mars, if you see an opponent take a card that clearly indicates a specific engine, you can adjust your own strategy to either compete or avoid that area.

Third, consider the randomness of the game. Some games have a high degree of luck in the opening (e.g., dice rolls in Catan, card draws in Dominion). In these cases, your opening should be robust to bad luck. Don't put all your eggs in one basket. In a game like Risk, the initial card draw can give you a continent bonus or not; you need to adapt your deployment accordingly.

Finally, consider the meta-game. If you're playing with the same group repeatedly, they will learn your tendencies. Vary your openings to keep them guessing. In chess, that's why players have a repertoire of different openings. In a board game group, if you always start by building a road to the same resource, your friends will start blocking you. Mix it up.

From Opening to Midgame: The Seamless Transition

The opening doesn't end with a specific turn count—it ends when the game's strategic landscape becomes clear. That transition is crucial. A good opening sets you up for a smooth transition into the midgame, where the real battles are fought.

One key to a smooth transition is to have a plan for your first few midgame moves. In chess, after the opening, you should have a plan for where your pieces will go and what you'll attack. In a eurogame, you should know which resources you need to acquire and which actions you'll take. If you've played a flexible opening, you can pivot based on what your opponents have done. If you've played a rigid opening, you might be stuck.

Another key is to recognize when the opening is over. In many games, the opening ends when the board starts to fill up, or when players have established their main positions. In Catan, it's when the first settlements and roads are placed and the dice start rolling. In a worker-placement game, it's when the first round ends and players have placed their workers. At that point, you need to shift from development to execution. Your opening should have given you the tools to execute your strategy.

Finally, don't be afraid to deviate from your opening plan if the situation demands it. The best players are those who can abandon a comfortable position for a better one. If your opening has put you in a strong position, but you see an opportunity to win the game in the midgame, take it. The opening is a means to an end, not an end in itself.

Conclusion

The opening is the most underrated phase of any strategy game. It's where you establish your position, set the tempo, and lay the groundwork for victory. By understanding the principles of control, efficiency, flexibility, and threat creation, you can start any game with confidence. By avoiding common mistakes like overextending, ignoring opponents, and being too passive, you'll stay in the driver's seat. And by adapting your opening to the players and the game at hand, you'll be ready for anything. Remember, the opening is not just a series of moves—it's a statement of intent. Make yours count. The next time you sit down to play, think about your first move as the first brushstroke on a canvas. Will it be a masterpiece or a mess? The choice is yours.
